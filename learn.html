The Rain Station Board: how it works, and the weather behind it

Written 2026-09-09 for a reader who wants to understand the board and learn the weather behind it. Read-only: this document describes what exists; it changes nothing. Every number in it comes from a file on the box that is named where the number appears.

1. The bet, in one paragraph

Kalshi lists a daily contract for each of 22 US airports: will the airport's official rain gauge record at least 0.01 inch of rain between midnight and midnight, local standard time? YES pays a dollar if it does. The contract settles on the airport's climate report, the same daily record the National Weather Service publishes, which weather.com carries. Two details decide most arguments: a TRACE (rain that wet the sensor but did not reach 0.01) settles NO, and the day ends at midnight standard time, which in summer is 1 AM on the civil clock.

So the whole question is physical and narrow: does one bucket at one airport tip once before the day ends. It is not "will it rain in New Orleans". It is "will the gauge at Louis Armstrong airport register 0.01". A downpour three miles away that misses the airport is a NO.

2. What a rain gauge actually does

  • The gauge is a tipping bucket next to the runway (the ASOS). Each tip is 0.01 inch. It reports every hour in the METAR, with a special report (SPECI) within minutes when rain starts or stops; the letters RAB38 in a METAR mean rain began at minute 38, RAE41 that it ended at minute 41.
  • P0000 in the hourly report means the bucket did not tip that hour: a trace at most. That is how Boston read at 5:54 PM on 2026-09-09 while rain was falling and weather.com's own observation said 0.01: one instrument counts tips, the other estimates.
  • Some airports also feed a 1-minute or 5-minute record (the board calls it the pulse). The board reads the government's aviation feed every 60 seconds, the MADIS 5-minute feed, and, when it is up, a 1-minute route.
  • The climate report that settles the contract is published after the day closes. Until it posts, the board says "no report yet" rather than guessing.

3. What the board shows, top to bottom

  • WHAT v2 SAYS TODAY: the physics model's calls. WILL NOT RAIN (under 10 percent) and WILL RAIN (80 percent and up) are the two ends that have a measured record, so they come first, each with the market price beside it and the hours when rain is still expected. MARKET DISAGREES lists the cities where the price and the model differ by 15 points or more, with the direction and the timing.
  • THE MAP: every gauge as a marker coloured by its physical state (locked, wet now, imminent, approaching, rain nearby, overdue, quiet, unknown), on satellite imagery, with the National Weather Service's MRMS radar on top. Tap a city and the map flies there and draws the gauge, a 6-mile ring, the storm cells with their arrival times, the neighbouring airports wet or dry, and the cameras where they physically stand.
  • EVERY GAUGE: all 22 as a list sorted by what needs attention, with filters for wet, due in 3 hours, and overdue.
  • The city page: one plain sentence first (what v2 says, what the market says, what the gauge says, what is coming and when), then the evidence stack: gauge, about-to-get-wet inputs, forecast, models, market, source health, raw.
  • SCORECARD: what settled today, and how often each source's numbers have been right, with the sample size on every line.

4. Reading radar the way the contract needs it

Radar sends out a beam and measures how much bounces back; dBZ is that echo strength, and it is a rough measure of how heavy the rain is at that spot. The board converts it into the only thing that matters here: how long that echo has to sit over the gauge before the bucket tips once.

what the map colour meansroughlyfor the gauge
TRACE, under 20 dBZ (blue)drizzle, or rain that evaporates on the way downoften never registers; a trace settles NO
0.01 SLOW, 20 to 29 (green)light rain, 0.02 to 0.05 inch an hourneeds 12 to 24 minutes of continuous rain over the gauge
0.01 LIKELY, 30 to 39 (yellow)steady rain3 to 12 minutes over the gauge
0.01 FAST, 40 to 49 (orange, red)a real showerabout a minute over the gauge
CORE, 50 and up (magenta)downpour or hailif it reaches the gauge, it is done

Three things a beginner should know. First, radar looks at the sky, not the ground: light echo aloft can dry out before it lands, which is why green over the gauge is a clock that has started, not a result. Second, a single radar dish reads its own ground clutter as rain close to the antenna; the board uses the national mosaic for the measurements and shows the single dish only for the close-up picture. Third, storm cells move: the board projects each cell's track, prints where it will pass closest to the gauge and when, and ages that estimate by the time since the radar scan, because a 30-minute-old ETA is not an ETA.

The distinction that cost real money this week: an amount forecast answers "how much rain will fall here", and 0.00 is a common honest answer even on a day a brief shower drops 0.01. The contract asks a different question, and the physics model is the only source on the board fitted to that question.

6. The physics model (v2), in plain words

v2 was built by jules, one of the two outside AI seats on this desk. It looks at the day's atmosphere over the airport and asks: does this air produce a shower that reaches this gauge? Its inputs are the classic ones a meteorologist would list:

  • CAPE, convective available potential energy: how much fuel the air has for thunderstorms.
  • Lifted index: how unstable the air column is (negative means storms can grow).
  • Precipitable water: how much moisture the whole column holds.
  • Relative humidity near the ground, cloud cover, the boundary layer depth (how deep the daytime mixing goes).
  • Pressure: the day's range and the fall, which is the signature of an approaching front.

It also carries a decay curve: as the day burns off dry, the chance of rain by midnight falls, and the model's rest-of-day number falls with it. That is why a city can read 27 percent at 6 AM and 15 percent at noon with no change in the sky.

The house rule on this desk: v2 is not amended. It is measured. Every claim about it below comes from a backtest on dates the model never saw, split by date so that one weather system cannot sit on both sides of the test.

7. How we know what to trust: the year-long test

How to read it. A station-day is one airport on one day. Dates is how many different calendar days the rows come from, which matters because on a big frontal day several airports rain together and count as one weather event, not four. The interval is the honest range around the hit rate: with 64 wet out of 1,904, the true rate is somewhere between 2.6 and 4.3 percent; with 1 of 1, the interval is 2.5 to 100 and the row tells you nothing. The rule: trust a row when its interval is narrow, and treat the interval, not the headline, as what you know.

What the year says: the low end is real across spring and summer (under 10 percent at 9 AM rains about 3 times in 100). The middle is honest and slightly conservative. Above 80 percent the model speaks rarely at 9 AM, so the year holds only 11 such rows, 9 of them wet; the 60 to 79 range is where the year's 'will rain' evidence sits, about 85 percent. In a 100-day summer-only test with prices, the under-5 bucket rained 3.9 percent of the time on days the market priced at 10.5 percent (research/2026-09-09_V2_HOLDOUT_BYDATE.md).

8. What went wrong this week, because the board learned from it

  • The desk told Colin Boston was 25 to 30 percent likely to rain at 9 AM while v2 said 91; the desk waved the model off as too few samples without measuring it. It rained. The measurement, run that afternoon, showed the model's 80-plus calls had been right 15 of 15 times on unseen summer dates. New house law: a model with a better measured record is never waved off as 'n=4'; the read carries every source's record beside its number.
  • The board's radar label and its 'calibrated' wording asserted things nothing had computed. Fixed: every label now says what it measures and how old it is; a stale input turns a station UNKNOWN rather than confident.
  • The collector could turn an unknown precipitation reading into 0.00, which reads as dry. Diagnosed by codex, approved by jules, fixed: unknown is now published as unknown.
